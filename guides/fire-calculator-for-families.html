Household plan

A FIRE calculator for a family

A family plan is still one portfolio, one spending number, and optional Social Security for two people. Childcare, a bigger home, and a second benefit belong in those fields. There is no separate input for each child.

Methodology

FIREcalc does not model a 529, a child tax credit, college tuition by year, or a dependent. Kids show up as spending. A spouse shows up as household income, household spending, and, in retirement, a second Social Security benefit you choose to turn on. That is enough to ask the real questions, and it is not a full tax return.

Put the kids in the spending box

While children are home, spending is high: housing, childcare, food, insurance. The savings calculator inflates that whole number with CPI every year. It will not cut spending when childcare ends. If you type today’s $110,000 budget as both the lifestyle and the 25× goal, the goal stays sized for a house that still includes daycare.

Split the two ideas. Keep today’s spending, including the kids, in the spending field, so the contribution is honest. Type a retirement portfolio sized for the spending you expect later, not for daycare. In the example, household income is $150,000 after tax, spending is $110,000, and the goal is $1,750,000. That goal is 25× $70,000, a guess at later spending. It is not 25× $110,000.

The contribution is $40,000 a year. Stocks are 70%. On 1,000 shuffled paths, seed 246813, the median path takes 15 years. The faster 10% are there by 12 years. The slower 10% take at least 19 years. Age 34 plus 15 years is 49. If the kids’ costs end sooner than that and you raise the contribution, the date moves earlier. The model will not do that for you. Lower the spending field yourself and run it again.

Two Social Security benefits

Social Security is off until you open “Social Security & other income” and check the boxes. A spouse has a separate benefit and a separate claiming age, 62 through 70. The amount you type is the benefit at full retirement age, 67. The engine scales it for the claiming age. It gets a cost-of-living adjustment from the second year. It does not start before the claiming age.

This retirement example is a household retiring at 60 with $1,750,000, spending $70,000 a year after tax, 60% stocks, a flat 15% tax, inflation-adjusted spending, and a 35-year horizon. Historical cycles only: a 35-year retirement has 16 complete windows in this table, from 1975–2009 through 1990–2024. The example benefits are $2,200 a month and $1,600 a month at age 67, claimed at 67. They are placeholders, not your statement from Social Security.

With those benefits on, 16 of 16 windows still had money at the end of every year. With both benefits off, this same $70,000 plan was also 16 of 16. The sample is kind, and 16 windows is a small set that starts in 1975. The benefits start to matter when spending is higher. At $100,000 of inflation-adjusted spending, Social Security off survived 15 of 16 windows and the example benefits survived 16 of 16. At $110,000, Social Security off survived 14 of 16 and the benefits still survived 16 of 16. That gap is the point of typing the real benefit. A success rate with Social Security on is not a portfolio-only test, and the result says so.

The flat 15% tax grosses spending up. $70,000 after tax is about $82,350 from the portfolio in the first year, before any benefit. Do not also subtract Social Security from the spending box. That counts it twice.

Taxes, a pension, and what is missing

  • Simple tax is one rate. Detailed tax uses 2025 federal brackets, the standard deduction, and married filing jointly or single. There is no child tax credit, no dependent care credit, and no education credit.
  • Social Security taxability in detailed mode uses married-filing-jointly thresholds even if you select single.
  • A pension is a nominal monthly amount. It does not get a cost-of-living adjustment. Part-time work can go in “other income” with a duration.
  • “Optimize withdrawal order” does not change the result. Separate account balances are not tracked.
  • Early-withdrawal penalties, RMDs, NIIT, IRMAA, and ACA premiums are not in the model. A family retiring before 59½ should not treat the success rate as a penalty-aware plan.

One spouse’s job, one portfolio, and one spending number is the shape of this tool. If the household has two very different accounts or a business, keep this as a sketch and do the ownership details elsewhere.

Questions people ask next

Where do I enter kids?

In annual spending. There is no per-child input, no college module, and no 529. If costs fall later, lower spending yourself. The savings model will not do it on a schedule.

How do I add a spouse’s Social Security?

In the retirement calculator, turn on “Include spouse’s Social Security” and type their benefit at age 67 plus a claiming age from 62 to 70. Leave it off if you do not want that income inside the success rate.

Should the savings goal be 25 times today’s spending?

Only if retirement spending will look like today’s spending. Childcare and a larger house often end. Size the goal from the spending you expect after that, and keep today’s higher spending in the contribution math.

Does a family plan run on a server?

No. The links open the calculator in your browser. The optional email form stores the share link you choose. It does not compute a success rate.
