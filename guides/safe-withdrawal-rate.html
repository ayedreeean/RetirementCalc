Spending rate

Safe withdrawal rate: 4%, 3.5%, and 3.25% on one portfolio

A safe withdrawal rate is the first-year spending, as a share of the portfolio, that you are willing to test with later years raised for inflation. On $1 million and a 60/40 mix, a 4% spend is $40,000. In this 1975–2024 sample, 21 of 21 thirty-year windows survived, and 985 of 1,000 shuffled 30-year paths survived. Stretch the horizon to 40 years and the shuffled count is 963 of 1,000. This is not a full-history Trinity study.

Methodology

Bengen’s 1994 paper and the Trinity study (Cooley, Hubbard, and Walz, 1998) are why people say “4%.” They looked at historical US retirements, including bad starts in the late 1960s, and an inflation-adjusted withdrawal. A 4% first year, raised with inflation after that, is the figure that work made famous for a 30-year retirement. Early retirees often test 3.5% or 3.25% because their horizon is longer than 30 years and because that older sample is not a forecast.

FIREcalc can run the arithmetic. It cannot rerun Trinity. The file starts in 1975.

Questions people ask next

Is 3.5% safer than 4% here?

On the shuffled paths, yes for this portfolio. At 40 years, 4% survived on 963 of 1,000 and 3.5% survived on 981 of 1,000. The historical windows survived at both rates, and those windows omit 1966 and 1973–74.

Why do all the historical windows survive?

Because every complete window in this file starts in 1975 or later. The published 4% research included worse start years than this table has. A perfect historical score here is not a repeat of that research.

What is the Trinity study?

The 1998 paper by Cooley, Hubbard, and Walz, and Bengen’s 1994 paper before it, studied historical US stock and bond cohorts and an inflation-adjusted withdrawal. People summarize that work as the 4% rule for a 30-year retirement. FIREcalc does not rerun their data set.

Does a 15% tax still count as 3.5%?

No. Simple tax grosses the withdrawal up. A rate test on this page uses a 0% tax, or you type a smaller spending number so the pre-tax draw is the rate you meant.
