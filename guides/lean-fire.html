Lean FIRE: how small a budget still holds up?

Lean FIRE means the spending you need in retirement is deliberately low, so the portfolio that covers it can be smaller. In this calculator that is an ordinary withdrawal test. $32,000 a year is 4% of $800,000. On a 45-year horizon, 6 of 6 complete windows kept a balance above zero at the end of every year. Shuffled years, seed 246813, are a different statistic: 957 of 1,000 shuffled paths kept a balance above zero for every year of the horizon.

Methodology

People use “lean FIRE” for a retirement spent well below a typical household budget. The label does not change the math. You still need a spending number you can live on, a portfolio, a mix of stocks and Treasuries, and a horizon long enough to cover the years before a later benefit might start. This example leaves Social Security off so the result is the portfolio alone.

The withdrawal example

Age 45, $800,000 invested, $32,000 of spending in the first year, raised with CPI after that. Stocks 70%, 10-year Treasuries 30%. Tax rate 0%, so the portfolio draw is $32,000, which is 4%. Horizon 45 years, to age 90. A retirement of this length has 6 complete windows in the 1975–2024 table. 6 of 6 complete windows kept a balance above zero at the end of every year. Those windows are 1975–2019 through 1980–2024. They do not include 1966 or 1973–74.

957 of 1,000 shuffled paths kept a balance above zero for every year of the horizon. The earliest of those failures ran out in year 18. At $28,000 of spending, which is 3.5% of the same $800,000, 983 of 1,000 shuffled paths kept a balance above zero for every year of the horizon. The lower rate failed less often on these paths. It is still this sample, not a promise.

Getting to $800,000

A second sketch is the savings path. Age 33, $45,000 invested, $68,000 of income, $40,000 of spending. The gap is $28,000 a year, and pay rises 1% a year above inflation. Working-life spending is higher than the $32,000 retirement budget. The goal is $800,000 in today’s dollars. Stocks are 80%.

On seed 246813, the median path takes 13 years, age 46. The faster 10% are there by 11 years, age 44. The slower 10% take at least 17 years, age 50. 1,000 of 1,000 shuffled paths reached the goal inside 50 years. 38 of 50 start years reach the goal before the sample ends. The median of all 50 start years is 14 years. The faster 10% are there by 12 years. Starts near 2024 have only a few years of data, so the slower tail includes windows that run out of history. Reaching the goal while you are still spending $40,000 does not mean the retirement at $32,000 has been tested. Run the withdrawal link once the balance is real.

Lean fails in this tool the same way any other plan fails: spending is high relative to the portfolio, the horizon is long, or the path draws a bad sequence. A small budget only helps if you can keep it. Fees, a glide path, and markets before 1975 are not in the engine. This is a planning sketch, not advice. Questions people ask next

Is there a separate lean FIRE calculator?

No. Type the small spending number and the portfolio that matches it. The engine is the same one the other guides use.

What counts as lean?

Whatever annual spending you can actually live on. This page uses $32,000 as an example, which is 4% of $800,000. It is not a definition of a lean lifestyle.

Why is a 45-year test thinner than a 30-year test?

The table runs from 1975 through 2024. A 45-year retirement has 6 complete windows. A 30-year retirement has 21. Shuffled years redraw from the same 50 rows and are not historical cohorts.

Does lean FIRE include Social Security?

Not in this example. Social Security stays off unless you turn it on. Turning it on means the success count is no longer a portfolio-only test.
