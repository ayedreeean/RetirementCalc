Social Security and early retirement

Retiring at 55 means twelve years before a claim at 67, and the portfolio pays the whole bill until then. On this example the benefit is an input of $2,000 a month at full retirement age, not a computed benefit. With Social Security off, 767 of 1,000 shuffled paths kept a balance above zero for every year of the horizon. Claiming at 62, 67, and 70 landed on 945, 949, and 944 of 1,000 shuffled paths. They are close to each other, and all three are well above the run with the benefit off.

Methodology

An early retirement and a Social Security claim are different dates. The portfolio covers every year before the claim, and a smaller or larger check afterward depending on the age you pick. The calculator will not estimate your benefit from earnings. You type the monthly amount at full retirement age. If you leave the box off, the success count is the portfolio alone, which is what the other guides on this site do unless they say otherwise.

The factors this tool actually uses

For someone whose full retirement age is 67, the Social Security Administration’s age-reduction table shows a benefit at 62 of 70% of the full amount. Delayed retirement credits for people born in 1943 or later are 8% a year, and the age-70 table for a full retirement age of 67 shows 124%. FIREcalc stores 0.70, 1.00, and 1.24. A typed $2,000 at full retirement age becomes $1,400 a month at 62, $2,000 at 67, and $2,480 at 70. Annual figures: $16,800, $24,000, and $29,760.

767 of 1,000 shuffled paths kept a balance above zero for every year of the horizon. That is the gap with no benefit at all. 16 of 16 complete windows kept a balance above zero at the end of every year. The historical file is 16 complete 35-year windows, and they all survived even with the benefit off, which is another reason not to quote this sample as a full history.

Turning the benefit on moved the shuffled count into the mid-900s at all three claiming ages. On this particular portfolio the three ages did not separate by much: 62 was 945 of 1,000, 67 was 949 of 1,000, and 70 was 944 of 1,000. Waiting until 70 pays a larger check and leaves the portfolio alone for eight more years than a claim at 62. On these paths, that trade was close. It is not a finding that one claiming age is right. The earnings test, spousal benefits, and taxation of benefits are not in the model. Social Security taxability in detailed tax mode uses married-filing-jointly thresholds even if you pick single.

Type your own full-retirement-age estimate. The $2,000 figure is an example input, not an average benefit. SSA’s planner is the place to estimate the check. This page only shows what the calculator does with a number you give it.

Questions people ask next

Are 62, 67, and 70 very different on this plan?

Not on the survival count. Shuffled paths: 945 of 1,000 at 62, 949 of 1,000 at 67, and 944 of 1,000 at 70. Off, the count is 767 of 1,000. A different portfolio could separate the claiming ages more.

Where do 70%, 100%, and 124% come from?

The Social Security Administration’s early-claim reduction and delayed retirement credits, for a full retirement age of 67. Claiming at 62 is a 30% reduction. Each year of delay after full retirement age adds 8%, up to age 70, which is 124%. FIREcalc uses those three factors and does not model months in between.

Does the check start at retirement?

No. The model pays nothing until the claiming age. From 55 to 62 this example has no Social Security income at all, even on the age-62 button.

Is the cost-of-living adjustment the real Social Security COLA?

No. Once the benefit has started, this tool raises it with the CPI rate on that year’s market row. That is not the Social Security Administration’s COLA formula.
