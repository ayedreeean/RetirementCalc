Retiring early with kids

Kids show up in this calculator as spending. There is no per-child field, no 529 balance, and no health-insurance premium. A household that puts a college set-aside and a health premium into the annual number is testing a higher withdrawal. On this example, $70,000 of base spending survived on 989 of 1,000 shuffled paths. $95,000, with $25,000 a year typed on top for those costs, survived on 939 of 1,000.

Methodology

A household retiring while children are still home has a higher bill and a messier one: childcare that ends, a college bill that arrives later, and health coverage that a job used to provide. The engine has one spending number for the whole horizon, raised with inflation every year. It will not drop the college line when the youngest finishes, and it will not price a marketplace plan.

Put the temporary costs in the number, and know that you did

Two honest ways to type a cost that will not last 40 years: raise spending for the whole retirement, which overstates the later years, or leave it out and keep a separate pile of money, which this calculator will not watch. This example does the first, and labels it.

The base budget is $70,000. The household then types $12,000 for health premiums and $13,000 as a flat college set-aside. Those two figures are assumptions for the sketch, not published premiums or tuition. The total is $95,000. Portfolio $2,200,000, age 50, 40 years, 60% stocks, tax 0%.

A 529 and the premium tax credit sit outside the engine

A section 529 plan is a separate account. IRS Topic 313 is the public description: contributions are not deductible on the federal return, earnings are not taxed while they stay in the program, and a distribution used for qualified education expenses is not taxed federally. If the 529 pays for college, leave the college dollars out of the retirement withdrawal. If it does not, the retirement portfolio is paying, and the spending number has to say so.

Health coverage from retirement until Medicare is a bill. The federal premium tax credit depends on household income. A conversion that raises income can shrink that credit in the same year. FIREcalc will not estimate either effect. The Social Security Administration tells people who delay a retirement benefit to sign up for Medicare at 65 anyway. The years before that are the gap you type as spending.

The third button turns on two benefits at 67: $2,100 a month and $1,400 a month, before the tool’s claiming-age factor. At 67 the factor is 1, so those are the amounts that enter the model, and they rise with the CPI series afterward. 980 of 1,000 shuffled paths kept a balance above zero for every year of the horizon. Quote that count as a plan with Social Security inside it. The family guide makes the same point with a different household.

This is a planning sketch, not advice about taxes, insurance, or college savings. Change the $95,000 if your premium or your tuition assumption is different. The result belongs to the number you leave in the form.

Questions people ask next

Where do the kids go in the form?

In annual spending. The family guide says the same thing. A 529, a college year, and a child who moves out are not separate inputs.

What is a 529 in this model?

Nothing. IRS Topic 313 describes qualified tuition programs: contributions are not federally deductible, and distributions used for qualified education expenses are not taxed federally. The calculator never sees that account. Spending the retirement portfolio on college means the tuition has to be inside the withdrawal you typed.

How do I handle health insurance before Medicare?

Add the premium you expect to pay to annual spending. The premium tax credit depends on household income, and a Roth conversion can raise that income. This tool does not compute a credit. Medicare eligibility is generally at 65, which leaves a gap if you retire earlier.

Did Social Security erase the extra spending?

Not entirely. With both benefits on at 67, the $95,000 plan survived on 980 of 1,000 shuffled paths, against 939 of 1,000 with the benefits off. The benefits are inputs, not a computed household benefit.
