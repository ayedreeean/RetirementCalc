Roth conversion ladder: the rule, and what this calculator can test

A Roth conversion ladder is a way some early retirees get at traditional IRA money before age 59½: convert a slice each year, pay income tax on the conversion, and withdraw that slice after its own five-year period. FIREcalc does not track accounts, conversion years, or the 10% additional tax. It can show what happens when the portfolio also pays a simple tax on the spending.

Methodology

Money in a traditional IRA is generally taxed when it comes out. A distribution before age 59½ is also generally subject to a 10% additional tax unless an exception applies. A conversion to a Roth IRA moves the money, and the amount you convert is included in income for that year. The planning habit called a ladder is to convert about one year of future spending each year, pay that income tax, and spend the converted dollars after the conversion’s own five-year period, instead of taking a penalized traditional withdrawal.

The two five-year rules

IRS Publication 590-B describes both clocks. A distribution is qualified, and earnings can come out tax-free, only after a five-year period that starts with the first Roth contribution and only if you are 59½, disabled, deceased, or using the first-home exception. Separately, each conversion has its own five-year period, starting on the first day of the tax year of that conversion. Take out the taxable portion of a conversion inside that period, while you are under 59½, and the 10% additional tax can apply to that portion even though you already included the conversion in income. Publication 590-B says these two periods are not necessarily the same.

The ordering rules in that publication, for a distribution that is not qualified, are: regular contributions, then conversion and rollover contributions oldest first, and within each conversion the taxable portion before the nontaxable portion, then earnings. A ladder spends the conversion layer, not the earnings. This page is a sketch of those public rules, not a tax opinion and not a worksheet for your Form 8606.

What the engine can show: tax paid from the portfolio

If the tax on a conversion is paid by selling from the same portfolio, the portfolio has to fund spending plus tax. Simple tax mode does a blunt version of that. It divides spending by 1 minus the rate. It does not know that a ladder often converts in a low-income year, and it does not know your bracket. The rates below are stresses you can type, not a computed tax.

Age 45, $1,000,000, $40,000 of spending, 60% stocks, 40-year horizon, Social Security off. At 0%, the draw is $40,000. 963 of 1,000 shuffled paths kept a balance above zero for every year of the horizon. 11 of 11 complete windows kept a balance above zero at the end of every year.

At a 12% simple rate the draw is $45,455. 919 of 1,000 shuffled paths kept a balance above zero for every year of the horizon. 11 of 11 complete windows kept a balance above zero at the end of every year. At 22% the draw is $51,282. 845 of 1,000 shuffled paths kept a balance above zero for every year of the horizon. 11 of 11 complete windows kept a balance above zero at the end of every year. Questions people ask next

Does FIREcalc run a Roth conversion ladder?

No. It does not keep a traditional balance and a Roth balance, it does not age a five-year clock, and the early-withdrawal penalty is not in the engine. The counts below are a flat tax on spending.

What is the five-year rule on a conversion?

IRS Publication 590-B says a separate five-year period applies to each conversion. Withdrawing the taxable part of that conversion inside the period can bring the 10% additional tax if you are under 59½. That clock is not the same five-year period that decides whether a distribution is qualified.

What order does a Roth distribution come out in?

Publication 590-B’s ordering rules for a nonqualified distribution are regular contributions first, then conversions and rollovers first-in first-out (taxable portion of each conversion first), then earnings.

Why did a 22% rate fail more often?

Because the portfolio draw rose from $40,000 to $51,282. On the shuffled paths the count moved from 963 of 1,000 to 845 of 1,000. That is the cost of paying tax from the portfolio in this simple model, not a computed ladder.
